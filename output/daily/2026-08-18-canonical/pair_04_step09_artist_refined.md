## ⛔ WHAT SHE REFUSES

**1. She refuses the pun.** `We hit it where it's screwed` was the draft hook of V2 and it was the first
thing I liked, which is exactly why it goes. It is a **double meaning**, and a double meaning is a toll —
the listener parses *screwed* twice and pays for the privilege. Worse, it invites a snigger, and a
snigger is the crowd being told a smart person wrote this. **THE REMARK TEST catches it and THE BIMBO
REGISTER convicts it:** the register that refuses to signal intelligence does not leave a clever thing at
the front of a chorus so you know somebody was awake.
> **CUT:** `We hit it where it's screwed` × 20 (five choruses × four lines)
> **NOW:** `We take it at the joint` — carpentry-exact, physical, plural, nothing to decode. **The title
> changes with it.**

**2. She refuses to look back in the doorway.** V1's draft signed off:
> `The room behind us is a floor with tape on it,`
> `and the tape says where it was.`

The second line is the whole run's forbidden facet arriving in six words. It **interprets** the empty room
instead of continuing the story, and *"where it was"* is a eulogy in the past tense. **THE MOURNER does
not have a seat today.**
> **CUT:** `and the tape says where it was.`
> **NOW:** `Nobody is looking at the floor.` — same length, opposite position, and it is the anti-elegy
> stated as an ordinary fact about four tired people walking to a van.

**3. She refuses to be sad on the record to prove the night mattered.** Two lines were doing it quietly:
> **CUT (V3 v3):** `I say yes. I do not look.` — reads as somebody who cannot bear to look at the thing
> she made. Suppression is grief wearing a hat.
> **NOW:** `I say yes. And I have a look.` — she looks at it properly, likes it, and it goes on the truck.
>
> **CUT (V4 v3):** `I'm the only one in here alone.` — self-pity, and **THE SECOND REFUSAL** is explicit:
> *she will not perform sadness to be taken seriously.*
> **NOW:** `I can hear the whole crew shout.` — the cost is still exactly the same (she is the one who
> goes back in), and it is now stated by what she can hear rather than by how she feels about it.

**4. She refuses to let the radiant announce itself.** Both V2 and V4 had radiants sitting **outside** the
ABAB scheme — `…break anything I built` and `…as long as I locked it` rhymed with nothing. In a
rhyme-heavy song a line that steps out of the scheme is the arrangement putting its hand up, which is the
device the whole of R3's second law retires.
> **CUT:** `…I could break anything I built.` → **NOW:** `…I was allowed to break what I make.` (rhymes *shake*)
> **CUT:** `…I could stay as long as I locked it.` → **NOW:** `…I could stay as long as I turned the key.` (rhymes *me*)
>
> ⭐ The radiant must be the plainest line in the song **and the least conspicuous**. It should slip past
> on the first listen and be the only thing left on the third.

## 🔪 WHAT SHE SHARPENS

**A. The chorus cue now carries the hole.** The draft chorus headers said only *room chant, stacked
thirds*. The pair's assigned device — **the downbeat that never lands** — was described in the style field
and nowhere in the lyric structure, which means the generator had one shot at it. Every chorus header now
ends **`one beat of open air before the last line`**, so the instruction sits at the exact bar it applies
to, four times a song. ⛔ It is still never mentioned **in a sung line**. Felt, not noticed.

**B. The seam gets its player named in the header as well as the lyric.** THE GRAIN LAW: a spec that fights
the generator gets smoothed. A seam mentioned once, in one field, is a spec fighting the generator. It is
now in the style field (*a second drum kit and a baritone saxophone arrive once, four bars behind*), in the
section header, **and** in the sung line (*Bex has got the baritone*), and the physical count-in is an SFX
cue on its own line. Four statements of one event. That is how a seam survives.

**C. Every song opens and closes on its own four-word chant.** *Work-lights up · On three · Heads up ·
Doors open.* THE HUM TEST is satisfied by the guitar figure, and this is the vocal equivalent: the last
word of each record is the first word of it. **RETURN IS FREE — spend more of it, not less.**

**D. The wolf lines were re-read side by side and one was weak.** V3's cost was originally carried only by
the running order, which is a decision, not an action. It now has the sentence that makes it cost
something out loud — **`I could have kept it to the last.`** — six plain words, said once, never revisited.
*(That is also the line that most needs the band not to notice it, and the band does not.)*

**E. Words-per-line was measured, not eyeballed.** Median across the four songs is **6, 6, 6 and 5 words**;
the longest non-radiant line in the entire pair is **ten words** (`is the last that anybody's asked about`
and `Everybody's aching, nobody's tired,`), and both survive because they are conversational rather than
literary. ⭐ **The four longest lines in the pair are the four radiants — 14 to 16 words — and that is the
only place length is spent.** *(Measured: `words_per_line` r = −0.202. **More lines, shorter lines.** Full
counts in step 10, read back out of the finished file.)*

## THE PAIR-LEVEL SELF-TESTS, RUN BEFORE STEP 10 IS WRITTEN

### ⭐ RETURN-SWAP
Closing lines swapped between all four, every direction. **Result: all twelve swaps break.**
* V1's `Nobody is looking at the floor.` has no floor to not-look-at in V2, V3 or V4 — three of the four
  songs end **outdoors**, and V1 is the only one whose last physical object is the room itself.
* V2's `Nell takes it off me and puts it in the van.` requires a bar in her hand, which only exists
  because she swung first — put it in V3 and the pronoun has no referent.
* V3's `Jax cut the corner off the good one.` requires a *good one*, which is V3's entire premise.
* V4's `Nobody has pulled out.` is the only ending where the vans are the subject rather than the setting.

**Four different objects, in four different people's hands, at four different distances from the building.**

### ⭐ WOLF-SWAP *(Fisher's falsification, run on my own four)*
Wolf lines swapped between all four. **Result: all twelve swaps break, and two break loudly.**
* V1 (`I called it over them. I missed / the last time anybody took a bow.`) needs an audience still in
  the building. **V2, V3 and V4 have an empty house.**
* V2 (`So I put the bar through my own part`) needs four people standing still waiting for her. In V1 the
  crew is already working; the line contradicts the verse above it.
* V3 (`I could have kept it to the last.`) needs her to own the running order. Nobody else's song has one.
* V4 (`I do the walk-round on my own.`) needs an empty building and a full car park **at the same time**,
  which is only true in the fourth song.

⚠️ **The honest caveat:** the four wolves are all *the same kind of wolf* — she takes an action and it
costs her — because that is what this pair was assigned. **The test is whether the lines are
interchangeable, and they are not.** The kind is shared with the concept; the events are not shared with
anything.

## THE ONE THING SHE IS STILL UNCOMFORTABLE ABOUT

**All four songs escalate by adding a name in the same place.** That is the pair's assigned architecture,
so it is not a defect — but four songs that all introduce their fourth body in the fourth verse is a
**shape** a listener could notice across a set. It is protected here by three things: the four rosters
share no names, the four escalations move in different directions (V1 bodies arrive *from* different parts
of the building, V2 bodies arrive *to wait*, V3 bodies arrive *to lift*, V4 bodies arrive *into a car
park*), and no other pair in the run is using the device at all. **Recorded, not repaired.** If the QA
judge finds it, it found something real.
