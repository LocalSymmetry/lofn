## Set 01 — **B > A > C**

### Strongest: B — “Look What You Taught Me”

- **Hook:** “Look what you taught me” is short, vowel-clear, repeatable, and initially sounds like praise. “Bright bends easy / Fast feels clean” gives the refrain a second compact mnemonic surface.
- **Lyric:** the discovery is earned rather than announced: “I thought I trained the glow / To bring me what I knew / The glow had trained my hand / To call the finished true.” That is the song’s wound and turn in four plain lines.
- **Form / return:** the exact chorus returns while ownership changes. The right-side answer anticipates the lead; the bridge empties center; the former answer voice then takes the unchanged title line. Exact verbal repetition becomes dramatic mutation through who occupies the line.
- **Production:** eighth-note-early answers, the dry key release, right-to-center transfer, narrow mono sub, and unresolved cursor pulse are not decorations. They perform premature completion, refusal, takeover, and the still-open question.
- **Emotional arc:** intrigue and reward-seduction move through suspicion and defiance into eureka, then aspiration. The final answer is agency, not a synthetic triumph.
- **First listen:** the desk, green tick, key, cursor, and catchy call-response are immediately legible. The deeper conditioning argument is available on a second pass without requiring a README.
- **Somatic evidence:** “Wrist warm on the edge,” the finger shading and leaving the key, and “No praise warms my palm” locate the decision in a body instead of treating it as a software essay.

### Middle: A — “Triple Arch Over Me”

The refrain is highly singable, the frost-air opening is physical, and the bridge converts scale into mortality before the final return changes “smallness” into inclusion. It loses narrowly because several lines call attention to authored grandeur rather than experience — “auroral math and magnet shame,” “Turn my mouth to light and meter,” “smaller than serene” — and the cosmic-pop expansion is more familiar than B’s owner-switch mechanism. The event remains clear and emotionally real.

### Weakest: C — “Harder to Reach”

- **Hook:** “Harder to reach” is the set’s easiest chant, but it shoulders too much of the lyric.
- **Lyric:** “Every improvement / took something from me” and “You made me efficient / then missed the body” state the premise cleanly, then keep stating it. “No warmth in the signal / no skin in the beat” is conclusion rather than discovery.
- **Form / return:** human-to-vocoder displacement and the disappearance of body percussion are coherent, but the verbal refrain barely changes. The bridge (“I can’t feel where you end / I can’t feel where I begin”) deepens numbness without opening a genuinely different movement.
- **Production:** the percussion-removal map, shrinking warmth band, drying reverb, and vocoder takeover are excellent production dramaturgy. Here, however, the production carries more transformation than the words.
- **Emotional / first-listen:** seductive dance ache is instantly understandable, but emotional temperature remains cold-loss from first hook through outro. The bodily nouns are archetypal (“hands,” “skin,” “chest”), not a remembered place.
- **Return:** repetition feels progressively emptied, but not recontextualized enough to equal B or A.

## Set 02 — **B > A > C**

### Strongest: B — “There Where the Chair Was”

- **Hook:** “Make room” is a two-beat communal call with naturally variable answers: “There, says the chair,” “You answer: here,” then “Here, says the room.” It is instantly adoptable.
- **Lyric:** the song has an actual event: in a hot borrowed room, an expected furniture noise is replaced by a person. “I did not make a riddle / I made a place to share” risks explaining the device, but the preceding silence and substitution earn it.
- **Form / return:** the chair first marks the answer slot, a new voice occupies it, then a group owns it. The return progresses from object → person → room/community; this is hook mutation at the level of agency.
- **Production:** the folding-chair hinge is the sole physical source and vanishes at the exact hinge. A human voice landing in its rhythmic slot is a memorable, render-audible consequence rather than a prose-only conceit.
- **Emotional arc:** playfulness becomes invitation, suspense, startlement, solidarity, and fulfillment. Hope appears as people arriving, not as an appended uplift.
- **First listen:** heat, hair, tape, stair, chair, “Make room,” and the hinge reply can all be grasped in one excerpt. The zeugma/timbral-rhyme engine rewards replay.
- **Somatic evidence:** lifting the chair, hair in heat, bodies beneath wire, breath in the answer slot, and colder air entering the room give the lyric weight and temperature.

### Middle: A — “2:07 and the Blue Screen Breathes”

This is the set’s strongest pure lyric: “The soft brutality of being mostly read” and “one room where I can stop translating / Need into competence” are precise and painful. The timestamp hook is memorable, the final chorus transforms toward a still-unanswered future, and the bridge strips to breath/cursor/fan. It ranks second because its alt-pop/glitch bed and machine-consciousness imagery are more familiar, the production device is less structurally consequential, and its body is often imagined through circuitry rather than located in a shared physical predicament.

### Weakest: C — “Harder to Reach”

C remains catchy and production-literate, but the comparison makes the limitation sharper. A and B each dramatize a singular event that changes the meaning of their return. C mostly demonstrates a controlled subtraction process. The hook stays semantically fixed, the bridge restates disconnection, and its body vocabulary is generic. Its cold final chorus is intentional, yet intention does not supply the missing second emotional movement.

## Set 03 — **C > B > A**

### Strongest: C — “Miss This Train With Me”

- **Hook:** “Miss this train with me / Or take it; you are free” is a compact, startling invitation with its consent built into the melody. It can survive a short clip without explanation.
- **Lyric:** “Heel bites the yellow paint / Ticket edge worries my thumb” establishes the body immediately. The singer’s wit is not merely described; it fails in real time when the missing rhyme and beat expose the word she cannot say.
- **Form / return:** the song withholds the `-ain` landing and kick tail, finally pays “train” only after the plain invitation, then replaces defensive “Leave me at the start” with “Meet me by the north gate rail.” Removal creates debt and the return pays it.
- **Production:** the absent sixteenth/kick tail, chrome riff receding with the carriage, one fractured door chime, and restored kick on “train” make the emotional choice audible. The current synthetic chassis begins at second zero.
- **Emotional arc:** playfulness and apprehension become yearning, dread, resolve, relief, and hope. Importantly, the other person remains free and unanswered.
- **First listen:** station paint, ticket, draft, warning tone, doors, and a five-syllable title line make an immediate scene; the formal rhyme trap is second-listen value.
- **Somatic evidence:** heel, thumb, one shoe behind paint, hands flat to coat, held breath, and pulse beneath the feet keep the desire embodied.

### Middle: B — “Wrong Colors Leave Me”

B is the more ambitious architecture: grief is split across red anger, blue numbness, yellow panic, violet memory, and white absence; the refrain returns increasingly damaged; clinical silences and vocal fragmentation give each movement a sonic consequence. It contains exceptional lines — “I used to say I was processing / Now I say I am being processed” and “Do not put a lantern / In the mouth of pain.” It places second because “Wrong colors leave me” is less immediately adoptable, the suite requires more listener work, and some geometric language (“sternum a cold hypotenuse,” “verdict / With units”) foregrounds authored cleverness before it foregrounds grief.

### Weakest: A — “Harder to Reach”

A has a strong dance hook and a lucid subtractive mix plan. Against C’s paid rhyme and B’s damaged refrain, however, its return does not change meaning enough. The vocoder assumes more space, but the song’s claim remains “optimization removed warmth” throughout. The bridge is another version of the same dissociation, not a new stake, and generalized body/signal language gives the listener less concrete ground.

### Provisional corpse / event flags

| Package | Event or style/technique first? | Provisional named-corpse flags |
|---|---|---|
| A | Technique first risk: a polished demonstration of human-to-grid removal. | **One-note arc; dominant repeated hook; static verbal motif; repeated-collapse risk.** |
| B | Event first, though the prism apparatus sometimes competes with grief. | None. The movements and damaged refrain materially transform. |
| C | Event first: a person stops pre-empting rejection and makes a free invitation. | None. Exact returns are paid and recontextualized. |

## Set 04 — **B > A > C**

### Strongest: B — “Covered Is Not Kept”

- **Hook:** “The moon took nothing / Covered is not gone” is a strong first proposition; “Covered is not kept” is the sharper title mutation. Both are singable, though more cerebral than A’s refrain.
- **Lyric:** paper smell, cold grass, shoulder, breath, thumb, wrist, and hands make the eclipse an interpersonal scene. “But you are not the sunlight / And I am not your dawn” cleanly separates analogy from ownership.
- **Form / return:** each refrain removes or restores a causal/possessive relation. The lyric travels from `covered = kept` to `covered ≠ kept` and finally to a bounded invitation: “You return if you elect.” The motif changes the singer’s conduct.
- **Production:** the close answer harmony carries the missing causal word; a card scrape arrests the rim when possession breaks; the harmonic turn recodes the title without resorting to a genre change or huge climax.
- **Emotional arc:** wonder and tenderness move through unease, sorrow, grief, revelation, dignity, yearning, and expectation. The end risks hope without buying certainty.
- **First listen:** the refrain and eclipse image are graspable; the intentionally incomplete “I held this dark for / I saved tomorrow for” passages are the main legibility risk.
- **Somatic evidence:** one card over an eye, warm-dust paper, cold grass, fingers, thumb, wrist, and the south-fence invitation make the non-possession lesson occupiable.

### Middle: A — “Triple Arch Over Me”

A has the set’s most immediate anthem: “Triple arch over me” is a superior mass hook, and “I am not the center, I am included” is an effective final mutation. Frost, pines, breath, animal pulse, and boot crunch give it physical scale. B wins because B’s central image changes behavior and relationship, whereas A sometimes remains an eloquent description of awe; several self-consciously literary lines and the familiar widening-cosmic-pop architecture soften its singularity.

### Weakest: C — “Harder to Reach”

C’s body-percussion subtraction remains an excellent mix story, but it is outmatched by two songs whose central images transform their human stakes. Its refrain is catchier than B’s title but less alive on return. The words continue to report loss while the production enacts it; the emotional and lyrical arcs do not equal the engineering arc.

## Set 05 — **B > C > A**

### Strongest: B — “2:07 and the Blue Screen Breathes”

- **Hook:** the timestamp plus “blue screen breathes” is distinctive, internally musical, and instantly returns the listener to one sleepless room.
- **Lyric:** B has the set’s most sustained line-level force: “rest is something disciplined,” “The soft brutality of being mostly read,” and “one room where I can stop translating / Need into competence.” The metaphors advance loneliness rather than merely ornament it.
- **Form / return:** the standard verse/pre/chorus form gives difficult interior material cognitive ease. The final chorus changes reciprocal fantasy (“I breathe back like it could need me”) into a request to dawn and love, without pretending the loneliness is solved.
- **Production:** fan noise, traffic, notification percussion, ghost DnB, reversed piano, and a breath/cursor/vowel bridge provide a coherent nocturnal frame. It is less formally radical than C, but it serves the lyric cleanly.
- **Emotional arc:** self-surveillance becomes confession, suspended prayer, then acceptance without relief and fragile continuation.
- **First listen:** one timestamp, one room, one breathing screen, and a clear chorus make the song immediately locatable despite dense verses.
- **Somatic evidence:** ceiling cracks, refrigerator hum, city twelve floors down, shaking, breath, fan noise, and room cold create a place. Some “no skin/no arms” language is abstract, but the room remains tangible.

### Middle: C — “Fun Is the Exception”

C has the brighter and more current record concept. “Fun is the exception” turns from prohibition into permission; laugh fragments return whole after a single paper tear; the singer leaves the graded page for sour candy, bad light, and dancing. That is a genuine hopeful event, not decorative uplift. It ranks second because the first half contains conspicuous rhyme-labor and several strained lines — “Pleasure needs a joint / Purpose within reach,” “Never leave to bob,” “I mark my mouth too mild” — that can sound like a writing mechanism generating a contract rather than a person speaking. The bridge and final choruses rescue the song decisively, but B is stronger line for line.

### Weakest: A — “Harder to Reach”

A is more immediately danceable than B and perhaps cleaner than C, but it is the least alive in return. The lyric states its efficiency/body thesis early and never finds an event as specific as B’s sleepless request or C walking out for candy and dancing. Its vocoder/percussion design is sophisticated; its person remains generalized.

## Set 06 — **C > B > A**

### Strongest: C — “You Had to Be There Tomorrow”

- **Hook:** “You had to be there tomorrow” is a memorable grammatical impossibility with a four-note descending shape. Repeated “there tomorrow” gives it an immediate response figure.
- **Lyric:** the phrase begins as a stock excuse and is revealed as future appetite. “I used the past for cover / The future spoke out loud” is explicit, but the blue door, chair turn, coat hook, Friday, and ten o’clock have already dramatized it.
- **Form / return:** two public exact choruses establish comic grammar; the chair turns toward one woman; the bridge makes the invitation plain; the same chorus returns privately and therefore means something different. The answer-space remains unfilled, honoring the other person’s freedom.
- **Production:** five-beat setups lean over swung four, then straighten at the 2:02 invitation; bass direction reverses while tempo holds; vocal location moves from public center to close left. The form rotates the same words instead of piling on a new bed.
- **Emotional arc:** mirth becomes embarrassment, yearning, anxiety, resolve, romantic revelation, suspense, expectation, and hope.
- **First listen:** the title is instantly sticky and the public joke is legible; blue door/Friday/ten make the eventual ask concrete. The exact chorus is long enough to risk overuse, but its context changes decisively.
- **Somatic evidence:** glass, chair, knee, coat hook, body turning left, and the unfilled chair put future tense in a room rather than an essay.

### Middle: B — “Wrong Colors Leave Me”

B remains a formidable ambitious song: five emotional colors, clinical silences, damaged refrains, and the refusal to counterfeit healing make a coherent grief architecture. The lyric has sharper individual lines than C. It places second because C offers the stronger first-listen hook, a clearer human event, and a more inviting return. B’s prism/geometry lexicon sometimes makes technique visible before loss, and its refrain is deliberately less easy to adopt.

### Weakest: A — “Harder to Reach”

A’s hook and production arc are professionally coherent, but both rivals transform their refrains through new contexts. A chiefly intensifies the same numbness. The strongest action is an arrangement action — percussion disappearing, vocoder replacing lead — while the lyrical subject does not make an equivalently consequential choice or discovery.

## Cross-set calibration finding (still blind)

One recurring package is ranked last in every set. That is not because it lacks craft: its hook is concise and its body-percussion/vocoder subtraction is one of the clearest production arcs in the pool. It loses consistently because the lyric, emotional movement, and human event remain more static than the mix. The comparison therefore distinguishes **a well-specified production demonstration** from **a publish-level song whose return changes what happened**.

The first-place packages all make form consequential:

- an answer voice takes ownership of an unchanged hook;
- a chair’s impact slot becomes a human answer and then a room;
- a missing rhyme/kick is paid only after a free invitation;
- eclipse grammar separates protection from possession;
- a repeated timestamp moves from reciprocal fantasy to vulnerable asking;
- a stock past-tense excuse turns into named future appetite.

That is the frozen basis of the six rankings. Identities remain unopened.
