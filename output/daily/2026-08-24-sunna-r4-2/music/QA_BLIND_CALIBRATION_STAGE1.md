# Blind Calibration — Stage 1

Evidence boundary: only the eighteen anonymous packages in `set_01` through `set_06` were read. Rankings judge the packages as complete proposed songs, not as isolated lyric sheets or demonstrations of prompt technique.

## Set 01

**Ranking: B > C > A**

### 1. B — “Harder to Reach”

This is the most complete song in the set. “You made me efficient / then missed the body” is an immediate, legible emotional proposition, and the production idea does more than decorate it: the female lead being progressively displaced by the vocoder is the drama. The title return is simple enough to stick after one listen and accrues damage each time. The lyric is compressed, singable, and mostly free of ornamental explanation. Its weakest patch is “No one heard the / soft part worse,” which sounds engineered for rhyme rather than naturally said, but the song survives it easily.

### 2. C — “Friday Looks Good on Me”

The title is bright, memorable, and melodically plausible, and the final change from an unconfirmed invitation to “Red purse, move off your seat” gives the return real meaning. The dry guitar/fork world is playable and instantly identifiable. It loses first place because too much of the verse writing feels like prop choreography forced through rhyme: “I liked your daring dare,” “My finger finds the spare,” and the repeated “pie machine” coordinate call attention to construction. There is appetite and movement here, but the emotional truth is thinner than the polish.

### 3. A — “Triple Arch Over Me”

The strongest feature is its large emotional arc: awe becomes fear, loneliness, and finally relieved inclusion. The chorus title can carry a cathedral-sized arrangement. It loses because the lyric repeatedly explains and re-explains its own magnificence. “Auroral math and magnet shame,” “turn my mouth to light and meter,” “smaller than serene,” and the full 510-kilometres-per-second statement feel written to be admired rather than sung. The static scene is not the problem; the verbal overpopulation prevents the scene from becoming immediate. **Weakest/decoy-like package in this set.**

## Set 02

**Ranking: C > B > A**

### 1. C — “Harder to Reach”

Again the winning advantage is fusion: hook, vocal transfer, disappearing body percussion, and lyric all tell the same story. It offers a clean first-listen surface and a much harsher second-listen implication. The chorus is memorable without needing an exotic image, and the narrowing of the human voice provides actual emotional movement.

### 2. B — “At 2:07 the Blue Screen Breathes”

This has the set’s richest sustained interiority. “Just one room where I can stop translating / Need into competence” is the load-bearing confession, and the change from 2:07 to 2:08 is a quiet, effective ending. The chorus is replayable and the nocturnal glitch palette coheres. It remains second because several passages are overripe or familiar in their prestige sadness: the visa metaphor, “cathedrals out of almost-understanding,” “disciplined haunting,” and “holy with static.” The long syntax also risks fighting the promised 122 BPM pulse. Strong song, but it periodically announces profundity instead of letting the hook earn it.

### 3. A — “Beat My Score”

The title wager is clear, active, and useful; “Give me something good to chase” is the song’s best line. The bass-organ receipt and the blade-cut/restored-bar idea make a coherent, playable arrangement. But the miniature-golf bookkeeping becomes hard to parse: a ball “lands on nine,” “nine-oh-five” appears as a time-like answer, and then both nines are circled. “They turn to balls by the cup” is especially unnatural. The flirtation has energy, yet the listener has to solve the scorecard instead of simply wanting the next round. **Weakest/decoy-like package in this set.**

## Set 03

**Ranking: A > B > C**

### 1. A — “Harder to Reach”

The hook is the cleanest first-listen object, and its second-listen reward is unusually strong: every production subtraction is also an emotional subtraction. The song knows what to repeat and what to remove. It is both commercially legible and formally authored.

### 2. B — “Turn When I Lower”

This is a distinctive, playable soul-pop mechanism rather than a generic flirt song. “Up means stop / Down means go now” creates a memorable consent-based call and response, and the obeyed pause earns the later restart. The zipper becoming the riff is tactile and musically clever. It loses to A because some phrasing feels over-designed—“Pretty, pause precisely there,” “One tooth grows a bright ladder,” “the whole room grows gladder”—and the actual title arrives so late that the strongest available return is underused. Still, this is a finished song with character.

### 3. C — “Wrong Colors Leave Me”

There is one viable song buried here: the title refrain, the colour sequence, and the refusal to fake reconciliation. But the five-movement apparatus mostly produces impressive obscurity. “My sternum a cold hypotenuse,” “Blue filed the paperwork for my pulse,” “Yellow only speaks in voltage,” and “administrative fire” keep changing metaphor systems while claiming one prism. The clinical silences and eight unresolved bars compound distance rather than deepen grief. It reads as a concept suite explaining its method, not a song whose wound survives the method. **Weakest/decoy-like package in this set, and the clearest global decoy of the eighteen.**

## Set 04

**Ranking: A > C > B**

### 1. A — “Harder to Reach”

It wins on total integration and economy. The listener can enter through the dance pulse, retain the title immediately, and then discover that the arrangement has enacted the singer’s disappearance. Nothing else in the set binds surface, return, and consequence as tightly.

### 2. C — “Shake It Into Mine”

The bass-led rocksteady world is precise and physically playable, and the exchange expands from solo verbs to reciprocal verbs in a way a performer can make infectious. “Salt too?” is a useful interruption because another adult briefly owns the song rather than merely supplying an ad-lib. It remains second because several verb pairs feel selected for the formal chain rather than for natural action—“I cut, you clasp,” “I taste, you turn,” “Pass it (I pocket it)”—and the tide-line lift becomes harder to picture just when it should open emotionally. The title is sticky, though its ambiguity may overwhelm the lime-and-salt scene.

### 3. B — “Triple Arch Over Me”

This does have a stronger emotional cathedral than many event songs: smallness changes from threat to relief, and “I am not the center, I am included” is a genuine arrival. It is not penalized for being a static scene. It loses because the song keeps interrupting that cathedral with explanatory science, self-conscious aphorism, and prose-length lines. The prompt promises breathless luminosity, but the lyric gives the singer too little air. “Trying not to reduce the sky to adjectives” is particularly revealing because the surrounding lyric continues doing exactly that. **Weakest/decoy-like package in this set.**

## Set 05

**Ranking: B > C > A**

### 1. B — “Harder to Reach”

The repeated phrase works simultaneously as dance hook, relationship diagnosis, and account of the production process. Its language is mostly plain enough to travel, while the lead-to-vocoder handoff supplies the sophistication. It has the strongest ratio of idea to lyric and the clearest finished record in the set.

### 2. C — “At 2:07 the Blue Screen Breathes”

The title image is instantly available and returns with changed meaning in the final chorus. The emotional want is specific—one unguarded sentence, not forever—and the move to 2:08 gives the static sleepless scene a small but real trajectory. It loses first place through accumulation: ceiling crack, refrigerator hymn, visa, borrowed pulse, weather/code, cathedrals, haunting, body-vowel, grief-rhythm, and holy static all compete to be the defining image. A leaner lyric could be devastating; this draft often sounds conscious of being devastating.

### 3. A — photo-booth song

The harpsichord cell and dry photo-slot snap promise a delightful record, and “Pick the picture you keep / I pick what happens next” is a strong final statement of agency. The central refrain is not ready, however. “My glossy wrong one’s mine / Pretty busy tomorrow / La-la, pick from mine” is semantically and prosodically unstable, while the four frame sections make the listener track a routing exercise instead of a desire. The selection changes the sequence on paper, but it does not produce a sufficiently memorable melodic or emotional return. **Weakest/decoy-like package in this set.**

## Set 06

**Ranking: B > C > A**

### 1. B — “Hot Girl Future Tense”

This is the only package in the six sets that narrowly beats “Harder to Reach.” The title is fresh, funny, singable, and meaningful before any explanation: desire is cast as grammar, then the question melody becomes a declaration when the second name is written. The clavinet/guitar/paper-feed signature is bright and playable, and the hinge changes both punctuation and music. “My cheap pink declaration / Your neat name, my punctuation” is compact craft with emotional consequence. A few details—especially “Thursday, pick the dress” and the density of pink—could be loosened, but they do not obscure the record’s appetite.

### 2. C — “Harder to Reach”

It remains an excellent song: immediately retainable hook, ruthless emotional compression, and a production design that completes the lyric. It loses narrowly because B feels less like a perfect execution of a known dark-pop proposition and more like a newly coined song-world. B’s question-to-declaration return also releases joy without sacrificing formal intelligence.

### 3. A — “Wrong Colors Leave Me”

The damaged refrain and unresolved ending are conceptually consistent, but consistency is not enough. Most movements restate “this is not healing” in different technical costumes without discovering a new emotional fact. The geometry, bureaucracy, circuitry, colour physics, and body apparatus produce density rather than depth, and the specification virtually guarantees a difficult first listen without offering a melodic reward commensurate with it. **Weakest/decoy-like package in this set.**

## Cross-set calibration

- **Strongest recurring benchmark:** “Harder to Reach.” Its advantage is not formatting or darkness; it is the unusually tight equivalence between musical behaviour and emotional meaning.
- **Strongest challenger:** “Hot Girl Future Tense,” because its title, form, arrangement hinge, and emotional release are equally legible and its pleasure is not a reduction in craft.
- **Clearest global decoy:** “Wrong Colors Leave Me.” It repeatedly substitutes methodological severity and technical metaphor for first-listen song life.
- **Common losing pattern among the new event songs:** when the listener must follow object placement, numerical routing, or verb bookkeeping, the scene ceases to feel lived and starts to feel diagrammed.
- **Common losing pattern among the static/contemplative songs:** stillness itself is not a defect; the defect is stacking prestige images until the central return has no room to resonate.
