## P01 — Ranking: B > A > C

### 1. Slot B — Somatic YES

This is the plate's strongest union of public hook, singer stake, and causal production. “Boop the future anyway” is short, hard-consonant, immediately adoptable, and frivolous without becoming weightless. The feeling actually moves: comic appetite becomes dread at “The star that made this face is dead,” admits that the joke was cover, risks a reciprocal invitation, survives the projector cut, and exits still wanting tomorrow. The title returns exactly enough to belong to the room; the clipped `oop` then pays removal debt by returning early as a high-third pickup rather than as decorative ear candy. The fixed beat-one kick/sub makes that transformation legible instead of converting every emotional event into a drop. Sunna is plainly at stake: she wants impossible touch, risks another adult's refusal, moves first, and leaves the answer free. The relay snap and abrupt `oop—and cut` give the ending a physical consequence and stop before relief becomes reassurance.

Named-corpse walk: not a one-note arc; no single repeated section carries the whole piece; the `oop` motif changes from hook fragment to harmonic pickup; the exact chorus accumulates new stakes rather than exhausting itself. Render risk remains that `boop` becomes childish, but the chest-led vocal, dead-star line, and adult invitation give the renderer strong counter-pressure.

### 2. Slot A — Somatic YES

“Touch the light with me” is a clean one-breath room hook, and the sliding patch of projected light gives consent a visible action instead of leaving it as policy. The emotional trajectory is coherent: tactile play becomes apprehension, censorship provokes defiance, the white-dwarf fact changes the singer's understanding of what she wants, and the open-palm turn converts contact into chosen nearness. The word-sized censor gap is sonically singular, especially because rhythm and sub remain intact while the surviving `igh` becomes a high fifth. The singer owns the want and the risk, and the ending's `igh` tail earns its bright cut by resolving the earlier blank.

It ranks below B because the lyric repeatedly explains the consent architecture (“Your distance stays your choice,” “Both choices in the white”) after the stage action already proved it. That semantic over-insurance slightly cools the flirt and makes the final long “I want” run feel engineered. Named-corpse walk: the arc turns; the refrain is not doing all the work; `igh` transforms audibly; repetition remains functional, though the title is close to exhaustion by the tail.

### 3. Slot C — Somatic YES

The legacy-shaped package still has real soul. “Triple arch over me” is a five-syllable prayer hook with powerful exact return, and the alpine scene is immediately physical: boot crunch, blue snow, pine, cold stone, ordinary pulse. Its emotional movement runs from breathless wonder through fear and loneliness to relief in non-central inclusion. The music has a recognizable threshold—near-silence into polyrhythmic pulse, low cello, glass timbres, then renewed scale—and the final chorus changes the hook's meaning from being dwarfed to being included. The ending is earned by the disappearance test in the bridge.

It ranks third as a text-to-render package because its production language stacks familiar cosmic signifiers, several phrases are self-consciously literary, the numeric fact is written as digits, and the singer responds by drawing a moral rather than taking a future-facing action. Named-corpse walk: it avoids all four corpses—the arc changes, the chorus has support, the triple-arch image changes meaning, and repetition is cumulative—but its older atmospheric notation gives the renderer less causal control than A or B.

## P02 — Ranking: C > A > B

The split `st` appears before explanation, becomes a percussion pickup, then conducts the fourth/seventh/octave top-voice ladder while the two-note bass walk remains fixed. That is genuine motif transformation, not a timbre swap. The emotional path moves from appetite and play through fear of being judged cheap, into a reciprocal unfinished sentence, then opens into present action. The singer's stake is precise: she may lose an adult who wants polish, but she presses the shutter before posing anyway. The crooked print, open curtain, and sentence ending at “might” keep the abstract idea embodied. The dry last flash looks forward rather than declaring completion.

Named-corpse walk: not one-note; no section monopolizes the payoff; `st` changes job repeatedly; the title's repetitions gain harmonic height and reciprocal meaning rather than becoming fatigue. The only material risk is gimmick—if the consonant stutter does not clearly conduct harmony, it will sound cute rather than causal.

### 2. Slot A — Somatic YES

Its future-tense hook changes into present-tense “We cross now,” while the top harmony rises over an unchanged swing pocket; that is an intelligible emotional and sonic consequence. The first `ah` returns as a roof, and the scuffed heel provides a warm, non-grandiose receipt. The ending earns hope by leaving the circle open rather than pretending incompletion disappeared.

Named-corpse walk: the arc changes, the hook does not carry the piece alone, `ah` gains a harmonic job, and repetition is mostly cumulative; the late stanza is the closest approach to exhaustion.

### 3. Slot B — Somatic NO

The title image is memorable and the singer's loneliness is real, but the package stays inside one nocturnal temperature: insomnia, digital yearning, and static holiness all reinforce the same ache. The chorus repeats effectively as text, yet the central motif never acquires a new musical job; the bridge simply removes drums and literalizes the production instruction. “2:08 now” changes the clock, not the singer's agency. The final chorus explains grief and asks for love without fragments, but no action makes that future more than a wish. The alt-pop/DnB haze, reversed piano, notifications, fan, and fractured harmonies are competent but interchangeable genre furniture.

Named-corpse walk: one-note emotional arc and motif-never-transforms both fire; the repeated chorus increasingly carries work the form has not earned, so repetition approaches exhaustion.

Counter-move: give the blinking cursor one irreversible musical and physical job at the turn—Sunna sends, deletes, or answers something—then make its click return as the changed ending mechanism.

## Cross-plate calibration

Across all six plates, the strongest packages make a small sound carry a changed causal job: `oop` becomes harmony, `st` conducts a top-voice ladder, a latch scar becomes a clock, “Here” opens a bounded pressure band, final `t` restarts the grid, and cloth squeak becomes crown. Those returns feel like songs because the singer's decision changes what the listener hears.

The common high-end risk is explanatory over-insurance. Several otherwise excellent packages restate consent, ownership, incompletion, or agency after the physical action has already made the point. Their render mechanics are strong enough that those thesis lines can become the new toll booth. The low-end decoys fail in the opposite direction: they have polished atmosphere and quotable lines, but no consequential act; color, static, cursor, and grief recur as emblems without acquiring new jobs. Ranking calibration therefore favored packages in which emotional turn, sonic event, and ending are the same event—not merely three adjacent descriptions of one.
