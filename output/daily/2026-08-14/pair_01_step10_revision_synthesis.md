**Last One, The Lights Are On**

# LINEAGE & CREDIT *(applies to all four variations)*

**Ska, rocksteady and two-tone are not textures. They are three named scenes with addresses, and two of
them are still working.**

- **SKA and ROCKSTEADY — Kingston, Jamaica, late 1950s into the 1960s.** The offbeat upstroke, the walking
  bass and the two-horn front line in these four prompts come from there and nowhere else. Point listeners
  **upstream** to the Kingston studio system that built them — Studio One and Treasure Isle — and to the
  house bands who played the figures, **The Skatalites** foremost among them. ⭐ **And specifically to the
  session horn players and bassists of those rooms, most of whom are uncredited on the records they made.**
  The horn line is the entire hook of these songs. Somebody's hands made that shape first, they were
  usually paid a flat session fee, and their name is usually not on the label. **That is exactly the credit
  a system like this forgets, which is why it is written first.**
- **TWO-TONE — Coventry, England, 1979, and the 2 Tone label.** Borrowed here as a **visitor**, never as
  the room. It earns its place in this song and not another because its founding gesture was explicitly
  about **who is allowed on the floor**, which is this pair's whole subject.
- **THE LIVING SCENE — contemporary ska-punk and the ongoing two-tone revival**, worldwide and mostly
  unsigned, playing rooms exactly like the one in this lyric. **Fusion with lineage, never capture.** We
  claim no lane, no genre name, and no first.

# ⭐ DESCRIBE-RENDER SELF-CHECK *(one capped pass)*

### V1 — what this prompt would actually produce
Four bars of trombone-and-baritone-sax with a room murmuring under it, then a fast, dry-sounding ska-punk
band with the vocal sitting *in* the mix rather than on top of it, talk-sung and unsweetened. The groove is
offbeat guitar and walking bass with a live kit that does not sound sampled; the count lands on the beat
and the room chants back. At roughly two thirds the whole band hits one low note and the tempo halves for
a single rocksteady passage, then the horns vanish and there is a bare stretch of chatter and counting.
**Name the one way this would render generic:** the phrase *ska-punk* is a strong enough prior that the
generator may deliver a bright, clean, tightly quantised horn-band with a sunny mixed-forward vocal — a
festival ska record, correct and weightless, where the crowd bed is polished off and the "wrong" horn
entry is silently repaired into a normal pickup. **The insurance is that the wrongness and the corona are
both in the words** — *"The horns come in wrong. Nobody fixes it"* is sung twice, and the bare count is
four separate lines. A smoothed render loses the texture and still cannot lose the count.

### V2
Trombone entering alone, early, cold, then a faster and messier version of the same band with hand-claps
that never quite sit right and a vocal that is more talk than tune. The join is a two-bar low unison and
one flicker of bright synth, then half-time and a room that is suddenly the loudest element.
**One way this renders generic:** *"a shade too fast"* plus punk energy could pull it into straight
skate-punk with a shouted gang chorus, which would flatten *Three. I come in on the three* into a generic
crowd hook and make her wrongness read as attitude rather than as a mistake she is keeping. **Insurance:**
the chorus's own third and fourth lines refuse it — *the horns are early, so am I / nobody fixes it,
neither do I* — and the exclude field bans the gang-shout stack directly.

### V3
An organ stab, eight bars of walking bass with people talking under it, then a slow, roomy ska with
brushes and a guitar line that runs against the vocal instead of with it. Low, level, half-spoken lead.
One low hit two-thirds through, then everything but the room drops out and the counting continues alone.
**One way this renders generic:** *quiet mid-tempo ska with air in it* is one step from a pleasant
lounge-reggae backing track — the flat organ tidied into tune, the countermelody turned into a tasteful
solo, and the whole thing becoming background. **Insurance:** the flatness is stated in the lyric (*the
organ is flat and it stays flat*), and the exclude field bans the lounge-jazz and smooth-sax routes by
name. ⚠️ **Honest flag:** V3 is the likeliest of the four to come back smoothed, which is why it is
ranked last inside the pair.

### V4
A wet towel, chairs, and the horn figure already at full sprint before anyone sings. Fast, hoarse, and
audibly late-night, with the room thinning behind the vocal. The join is a short, violent drum-and-guitar
breakdown, two bars, and then half-time and bar staff talking over a bare count.
**One way this renders generic:** *"last song of the night"* is a stadium-anthem attractor — the generator
wants to give it a key change, a crowd cheer and a triumphant last chorus, which would be **vindication
reintroduced by the machine after step 09 killed it in the writing**. **Insurance:** the exclude field
bans the key change, the modulation, the crowd sample, the applause and the false ending explicitly, and
the lyric's last three lines are a room that has already gone.

**Self-repair pass: one, taken.** V4's style prompt originally ended on the horns disappearing, which left
the count as a production instruction rather than a stated one. **Added: *"The count is never quantised."***
That is the only repair; the loop is closed and not re-entered.

## Self-critique

**The repeated-line ratio is between 0.628 and 0.646, against a floor of 0.20 — roughly three times over.**
That is not an accident and it is not padding: the answering half of every stanza is a fixed two-line
return, by design, and the answer *is* the device. But it is worth saying plainly — these are songs with a
small number of distinct lines doing a large number of jobs. **If one of them is thin, the measurement
will not tell you.** The numbers say the return floor is cleared three times over; they do not say the
song is good.

**The four sit in one room with one device.** That is the assignment — one pair, four angles — and the
angles are genuinely different objects: a count, a wrong entrance, a distance, and a closing time. They
are still four takes of one night, and **whichever one ships should ship alone.**

**V3 is the honest weak point** and I have said so above rather than letting a judge find it: the quietest
prompt is the most smoothable, and *gorgeously restrained beats* is a phrase a generator will happily
resolve into background music. It is ranked last inside the pair for that reason and not for its writing.

**And the thing none of these numbers touch:** whether *"Not the brave one. Just the two"* is funny. If it
is not, this is a well-measured diagram with horns on it.
