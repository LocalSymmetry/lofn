## §1 — ⭐ D3 DECLARATION: THE TWO LINES, NAMED BY INTERVAL, BEFORE A WORD OF LYRIC EXISTED

> **The Marsalis seat's unwithdrawn condition** (ICB, CROSS-PANEL SYNTHESIS D3 and *THE OBJECTIONS THAT WERE NOT RESOLVED*): *"show me the two lines, named by interval, before you write any words."* This section was written first. It is a **refinement** of `step04_medium.md`'s coordinator-layer declaration (`shouted pre-chorus ↔ sung chant · fourth`), not a reinvention.

**Key: E Dorian. Tempo 150–156 BPM depending on variation. One voice — a male tenor — sings both lines.**

| | **LINE A — THE SUNG CHANT** | **LINE B — THE SHOUTED PRE-CHORUS** |
|---|---|---|
| Words | `Put me down` (the fixed three-word answer) | `PUT IT DOWN` |
| Contour | **rises**: F♯4 → A4 → **B4** | **falls**: B4 → A4 → **E4** |
| Syllable placement | Put = F♯4 · me = A4 · **down = B4** | PUT = B4 · IT = A4 · **DOWN = E4** |
| Timbre class | one male tenor, chest, sung | the same male tenor, shouted |
| Register | both live in the fourth octave |
| Delivery | cracking at the top | flat, thrown through an open door |

**THE INTERVAL, both sides of the crossing:**
- **Before**: Line B starts a **perfect fourth ABOVE** where Line A starts (B4 over F♯4).
- **After**: Line B ends a **perfect fourth BELOW** where Line A ends (E4 under B4).
- **The crossing point is A4** — the *middle syllable of both lines*, on the same beat, one ascending and one descending. **Neither bends. Neither is transposed. Neither is altered.**

⭐ **Why they are mistakable for one voice getting louder:** same singer, same octave, a fourth apart, and the two texts are *the same three words with one word changed* — `Put me down` / `PUT IT DOWN`. The FALSE INTERSECTION is satisfied at the level of the words themselves, not only the pitches: two elements of the same family, entering side by side, easily mistaken for one another.

**⭐ THE SONG DOES NOT GET BIGGER AT THE CROSSING — and here is the structural reason, not a mix instruction.** At the moment of the crossing the *loudest* element (the shout) is **descending** while the sung line is ascending. Total energy is held constant by contrary motion, in the notes. Nothing is added. **One crossing per song, not two** (L38, N = 1).

**Where the crossing is legible without any dynamics at all:** the shout's position **on the page** moves. In Pre-Chorus 1 and the first half of Chant 2 the shout is printed *above* each sung line. After the crossing it is printed *below* every sung line, and it stays below until it stops. **The trick is recoverable from word order alone**, which is the one thing a generator always honours.

**And the word that carries it:** in the sung line the word **"down" lands on the highest note in the song (B4)**. In the shouted line the word **"DOWN" lands on the lowest (E4)**. The same word, in opposite directions, in the same room.

The room means **add my name to the rota**. It is an ordinary British domestic idiom for exactly this situation and nothing else.

## §8 — SELF-CRITIQUE

**The genuinely weak point is F4 against F1.** The three-word answer has to be legible as ordinary rota-speak in the first thirty seconds *and* carry a second meaning the room cannot hear. Those pull in opposite directions: the more idiomatic the phrase, the more invisible the second reading; the more pointed the second reading, the more the singer looks like he is in on it and D1 breaks. `Put me down` sits where it sits because it is the *only* phrase found that is genuinely the first thing a British person says to a sign-up sheet **and** genuinely a put-down. If it reads as a pun rather than as speech, this pair fails, and it fails at F4 rather than anywhere I can measure.

**Second weakness, recorded so it is not discovered later:** there is a third, unwanted reading of the phrase (the veterinary one). It is mitigated by never letting the words stand near an animal, an illness or a euphemism — the phrase is always adjacent to a sheet, a pen, a name or a chair — but it is not eliminated, and a listener who hears it will hear something bleaker than the song intends.

**Third:** the pair carries the run's INDIGNATION requirement inside the ACCESSIBLE arm, where the temptation is to buy accessibility with volume. Every place that temptation appeared, the answer was more *return* — paired rhymes, an answerable call, a byte-identical shout — and not more level. Whether that reads as accessible or merely as loud is not decidable from here.
